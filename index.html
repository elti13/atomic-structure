LESSON 01

What is an atom?

Everything with mass is built from atoms. An atom is the smallest unit of an element that still behaves like that element.

Three particles, two locations

An atom has a dense center called the nucleus, holding protons and neutrons. Electrons occupy the space around it. Charge is what separates the three.

p⁺
Charge +1

In the nucleus. Mass ≈ 1 amu.

n⁰
Charge 0

In the nucleus. Mass ≈ 1 amu.

e⁻
Charge −1

Outside the nucleus. Nearly massless.

Scale of the nucleus

The nucleus takes up roughly one hundredth of the atom's width. Almost all of an atom's volume is empty space; almost all of its mass sits in that speck at the center.

Two models, one atom. Drawing electrons on neat rings is a starting picture, not the real thing. Electrons do not travel fixed circular paths. The modern description gives regions of space where an electron is likely to be found — a probability cloud, not an orbit.

LESSON 02

Inside the atom

Nobody has ever seen an atom directly. The current model was assembled from experiments, each one correcting the last.

How the model changed

Counting the particles

NeutronsUncharged nuclear particlesn⁰ = A − Z

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have 6 protons; carbon-14 carries two extra neutrons.

The atomic mass printed on the periodic table is a weighted average: each isotope's mass multiplied by its natural abundance, all added together.

Mass spectroscopy — identifying isotopes

A mass spectrometer separates the atoms of an element by isotope. Each peak is one isotope; the height of the peak is how common that isotope is. Averaging those masses by abundance gives 35.45 for chlorine.

LESSON 03

Electron configuration

Electrons are not scattered at random. They fill a strict hierarchy, and that hierarchy is what the whole periodic table is built on.

The hierarchy

An energy level holds sublevels. A sublevel holds orbitals. An orbital holds at most two electrons.

energy level → sublevel → orbital → electrons

Each orbital holds two electrons, so the capacity of a sublevel is twice its number of orbitals: s holds 2, p holds 6, d holds 10, f holds 14.

Electrons occupy the lowest available sublevel before any higher one. Adding electrons out of order gives an atom that is not in its ground state.

Rule 3 — Pauli exclusion. No orbital holds more than two electrons, and those two must have opposite spins. That is why the paired boxes above read ↑↓ and never ↑↑.

Worked example — calcium

Calcium has 20 electrons. Filling from the lowest sublevel upward:

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s²

Counting the superscripts: 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 2 = 20. The shorthand replaces everything up to the previous noble gas with its symbol in brackets: [Ar] 4s². Core electrons are the inner ones; valence electrons are the outer ones that do the chemistry.

Evidence: photoelectron spectroscopy

X-rays knock electrons out of an atom, and the instrument measures how tightly each one was held. The resulting spectrum matches the predicted configuration exactly.

Peak height gives the number of electrons in that subshell. Peaks further left were held more tightly, meaning they sat closer to the nucleus in a lower energy level. Oxygen shows three peaks in a 2 : 2 : 4 ratio — exactly 1s² 2s² 2p⁴.

LESSON 04

Light & energy

Nearly everything known about electrons came from watching how atoms absorb and release light. Modern atomic theory grew out of studying radiation meeting matter.

Wavelength and frequency

520 nm
Frequency ν = c / λPhoton energy E = hνRegion

Shortening the wavelength packs more cycles into each second, so frequency rises — and with it, energy. Stretching the wavelength does the reverse.

The relationships

Wavelength (λ) is the distance between two crests. Frequency (ν) is the number of cycles passing a point each second. Multiplying them gives the wave's speed, and for light in a vacuum that speed is fixed:

c = νλ    c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s

Because c never changes, λ and ν are locked together: if one goes up the other must come down. Visible light runs from about 400 nm (violet) to 750 nm (red).

Energy arrives in packets

Planck found that atoms cannot absorb or release just any amount of energy. Energy comes in fixed minimum amounts called quanta, and the size of each quantum depends on frequency:

ΔE = hν    h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s

The photoelectric effect confirmed it. Shining light on a metal ejects electrons only above a threshold frequency — dim light of the right frequency works, while intense light of too low a frequency does nothing. That behavior fits particles of light, each carrying one quantum of energy, rather than a continuous wave. Those particles are photons.

LESSON 05

Coulomb's law

One question sits underneath every trend in this unit: how strongly does the nucleus hold on to a particular electron?

Why electrons stay

A nucleus is positive and an electron is negative, so they attract. Two charges of the same sign repel instead. The strength of that pull or push depends on only two things: how big the charges are, and how far apart they sit.

Charge and distance

+1
+1
180 pm

Relative force of attraction: 1.00

Distance is squared in the denominator, so it dominates. Doubling the charge doubles the force; doubling the distance cuts it to a quarter.

The equation

F = k · q₁q₂ / r²
FForce of attraction or repulsion
kA constant
q₁, q₂The charges on the two bodies
rThe distance between them

Force increases when the charges get larger, and decreases when the distance grows. Opposite charges give attraction; like charges give repulsion.

LESSON 06

Effective nuclear charge

Sodium has 11 protons. Its outermost electron does not behave as though it is being pulled by 11 protons. Something is getting in the way.

Shielding

Between the outer electron and the nucleus sit the inner electrons. They are negative, so they push the outer electron away, partly cancelling the nuclear pull. The outer electron ends up feeling a reduced charge.

What the outer electron sees

The full +11 pull leaves the nucleus, but the inner electrons cancel most of it before it reaches the outer electron. What survives is the effective nuclear charge.

The equation

Zeff = Z − S

Z is the number of protons. S is the shielding contributed by the other electrons. Zeff is what the electron in question actually feels. A rough estimate treats S as the number of core electrons, so sodium's valence electron feels about 11 − 10 = +1.

Slater's rules — a closer estimate

Slater's rules assign each other electron a shielding value based on where it sits relative to the electron being studied. The electron itself is never counted — nothing shields an electron from itself.

Position of the other electronShielding contributed
Same group as the electron studied0.35 each (0.30 in 1s)
One shell inside (n − 1)0.85 each
Two or more shells inside1.00 each

Groups are written as (1s)(2s,2p)(3s,3p)(3d)(4s,4p)(4d)(4f)(5s,5p). Electrons in higher groups than the one studied contribute nothing.

Separate rule for d and f electrons. When the electron studied is in a d or f sublevel, every electron in a group to its left counts as a full 1.00 — the 0.85 tier does not apply.

LESSON 07

Periodic trends

The table's patterns are not coincidences. Every one of them comes from three quantities: nuclear charge, distance, and shielding.

Trends across periods 1–4

Why radius increases down a group

Each step down adds a new energy level. The outermost electrons sit in a shell further from the nucleus, with more inner electrons shielding them. Greater distance and greater shielding both weaken the attraction, so the atom is larger.

Why radius decreases across a period

Moving right adds protons, but the added electrons go into the same shell where they shield each other poorly. The nucleus pulls harder on every electron, drawing the whole cloud inward.

Why ionization energy increases across a period

Ionization energy is the energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom in its ground state. A stronger effective nuclear charge and a smaller radius mean the electron is held more tightly, so more energy is required to take it away. Each successive removal costs more than the last: IE₁ < IE₂ < IE₃.

Electron affinity and electronegativity

Electron affinity is the energy released when an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom, forming a negative ion. It generally becomes more exothermic left to right across a row, with two notable exceptions to that trend. Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom pulls shared electrons away from another atom, and follows a similar direction to ionization energy.

LESSON 08

Ions & ionic size

An atom becomes an ion when its electron count stops matching its proton count. The proton count never changes — that would make it a different element.

Gaining and losing electrons

Na

Protons

Electrons

Removing a negative electron leaves an excess of protons, so the ion is positive. Adding one gives an excess of electrons, so the ion is negative.

Size follows the electron count

A cation is smaller than its parent atom. Losing an electron reduces the repulsion among the remaining electrons and often empties the outermost shell entirely, so the rest are drawn in tighter.

An anion is larger than its parent atom. Extra electrons crowd the same shell, repel each other, and push the cloud outward while the nuclear charge stays the same.

cation < neutral atom < anion

Ionic size depends on three things: the nuclear charge, the number of electrons, and the orbitals those electrons occupy.

Isoelectronic species — same electrons, different nuclei

Every species here has 10 electrons. The only difference is the number of protons. More protons pull the same ten electrons in harder, so the radius shrinks steadily from left to right. Mg²⁺ is the smallest, O²⁻ the largest.

LESSON 09

Metals & nonmetals

Whether an element gives electrons away or takes them decides almost everything else about it — how it looks, how it conducts, and what its oxide does in water.

Metals

  • Conduct heat and electricity well
  • Malleable — can be hammered into sheets
  • Ductile — can be drawn into wire
  • Shiny lustre, various colors
  • Tend to lose electrons and form cations in aqueous solution
  • Most metal oxides are ionic solids that are basic

Nonmetals

  • Poor conductors
  • Usually brittle; some hard, some soft
  • No metallic lustre
  • Tend to gain electrons, reaching a noble gas configuration
  • Form anions or oxyanions in aqueous solution
  • Most nonmetal oxides are molecular substances that form acidic solutions

The oxide rule is the one to remember. Metal oxide plus water gives a base; nonmetal oxide plus water gives an acid. It links an element's position on the table directly to the behavior of its compounds.

Metal charges worth memorizing

Most main-group metals take a predictable charge, but these four come up constantly and do not follow an obvious pattern:

Al³⁺   Zn²⁺   Cd²⁺   Ag⁺

A metal oxide in acid

Scandium oxide is basic, so it reacts with nitric acid to give a salt and water:

LESSON 10

Chemistry calculations

Atoms are far too small to count one at a time, so chemistry counts them in bulk — and then converts that count into a mass that a balance can read.

The mole is a counting unit

A dozen is 12 of something. A mole is a much larger fixed number of something:

1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles

Molar mass connects that count to mass. It is the mass of one mole of a substance, in grams, and it is the number printed on the periodic table.

MM = m / n

Dimensional analysis

Multiply the known quantity by a conversion factor arranged so the unwanted unit sits in the denominator and cancels.

Empirical formula — the simplest ratio

A molecular formula counts the atoms in one molecule. An empirical formula reduces that count to its simplest whole-number ratio. Glucose has 6 carbons, 12 hydrogens and 6 oxygens; dividing every subscript by 6 gives:

C₆H₁₂O₆ → CH₂O

Going the other way needs the molar mass. Divide the compound's molar mass by the empirical formula's mass, then multiply every subscript by that whole number.

Percent composition

The fraction of a compound's mass contributed by one element.

% element = (mass of element ÷ molar mass of compound) × 100

08 — EXPLANATION

Using Coulomb's law, explain why the first ionization energy of magnesium is higher than that of sodium.

Sodium has 11 protons and magnesium has 12, and both hold their valence electrons in the third shell. Because those electrons occupy the same shell, they shield each other weakly, so magnesium's extra proton raises the effective nuclear charge felt by its valence electron rather than being cancelled out.

Coulomb's law says the force of attraction rises as charge rises and falls as distance grows. Magnesium's larger effective charge acts over a slightly shorter distance, since the stronger pull also contracts the atom. Both factors increase the attraction between nucleus and valence electron.

A more strongly held electron takes more energy to remove, so magnesium has the higher first ionization energy.

The through-line. Charge, distance, and shielding explain radius, ionization energy, electron affinity, electronegativity, and ionic size. Every trend in this unit is the same argument applied to a different question.
